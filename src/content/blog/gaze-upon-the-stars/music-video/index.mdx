## Tangent: Unreal Engine woes

### Linuxn't

Unreal Editor 5.7 is the first version to use Wayland on Linux by default, and it might just have
the worst Wayland support I've ever seen in the over 2 years I've been daily driving Linux. There's
[a post about it](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/unreal-editor-5-7-ui-interaction-is-broken-on-linux-for-3-month-already/2652325)
on the Unreal Engine forums if you want to learn more.

To fix this, I first had to launch Unreal Engine with the `SDL_VIDEODRIVER=x11` environment
variable, to force it to go back to X11 and run under Xwayland. That alone wasn't enough though,
because it broke a bunch of other things instead. Thankfully I found a fix in
[that same forum thread](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/unreal-editor-5-7-ui-interaction-is-broken-on-linux-for-3-month-already/2652325/61),
although it's still not perfect.

<small>

### Gazing upon the Stars

Since the song is titled "gaze upon the stars", obviously the sky needed some stars to gaze at. And
for some reason, UE5's default sky just doesn't have any of them, which is unfortunate.

You might say you can drop in the legacy sky blueprint left over from older versions of Unreal,
which _does_ have stars, but those are **not animated**. And that's just lame. They do offer another
solution, the _Celestial Vault_ plugin, but it completely broke my lighting setup, so I couldn't use
it.

I initially used a Fab asset called _Good Sky_ to handle the sky, which did have animated stars.
However, when I posted an
[early version of the music video](https://discord.com/channels/1414500915200917597/1482484409595855041/1482484409595855041)
in the Home Paige Discord server, people craved for more. They wanted shooting stars and
constellations. And Good Sky wasn't going to cut it for that. So I ended up doing it myself, by
making **my own sky material**.

##### Twinkle, Twinkle

Cool. Now we have moving stars. But here on Earth, stars don't just appear to move, they also
[twinkle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twinkling). So how do we do that? Why, with more noise, of
course!

The graph is essentially the same as the above, just with different parameters. So instead of adding
another yet image <small>(this article already has a lot of them, which is not ideal for the GitHub
repository size)</small>, here's a complete list of the things I changed for the twinkle noise:

- Inputs:
  - Speed → `-1, -0.6, 0.6, 0.125`
  - Scale → `22`
  - Density → `0.87`
- Noise parameters:
  - Function → `Simplex - Texture Based`
  - Level Scale → `1.4`
- There's also a **1-x** node after the last **Saturate** node, just before the final output.

You then multiply the stars with this new noise, et voilà, twinkling stars! Okay, there's a bit more
going on afterwards, but I'll save that for the end of this section.

##### Colouring them in

Stars are also [not just pure white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_index). They can have a
whole range of temperatures, resulting in colours from orange to white to blue. It is pretty subtle
though, which is why people often just make stars white. Most of us simply don't care all that much.

I do care, though. And that means it's time to add another layer of noise in the mix. I definitely
didn't use the most correct approach for this, but it's good enough for government work:

<MainImage src={import("./assets/images/M_NightSky-ColorMap.avif")} alt="" createdIn="2026">
	There is a lot of math involved here to try and get a more reasonable colour temperature range.
</MainImage>

And here's the function for getting the colour from a temperature (`MF_ColorFromTemperature`), based
on
[this algorithm from Tanner Helland](https://tannerhelland.com/2012/09/18/convert-temperature-rgb-algorithm-code.html).
It probably didn't need to be this complex, but whatever.

<MainImage src={import("./assets/images/MF_ColorFromTemperature.avif")} alt="" createdIn="2026" />

Again, you multiply the final colour noise with the twinkling stars to make them coloured. Simple!
The end result is barely noticeable though, and in hindsight I could've saturated the colours a bit.
Oh, well. It's still a bit better than just plain white.

##### Stack the Layers

We start off with -- you guessed it! -- more noise. Let's go over each layer here one by one.

1. We've got a course, primary noise layer.
2. That layer is then exponentiated a ton and then inverted, in order to turn it into a few
   occasional speckles.
3. Then we have a finer secondary noise layer for some extra detail.
4. Finally, that gets multiplied with the primary noise to break it up a bit, and is then blended
   with the speckles to get our final, combined noise layer.

<MainImage src={import("./assets/images/M_Concreteish-NoiseLayers.avif")} alt="" createdIn="2026" />

There are 2 more material functions at play here. The first (`MF_Noise`) is a simple helper function
to make some noise:

<MainImage src={import("./assets/images/MF_Noise.avif")} alt="" createdIn="2026" />

And the second (`MF_Sigmoid`) is a crude little
[sigmoid function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmoid_function); a function that gets closer to 1
as the input increases, but never actually reaches it.

Technically it should do the same for negative numbers but approaching -1 instead, but I just
skipped it since there are no negative numbers to be seen here. If there were, something must have
gone seriously wrong.

<MainImage src={import("./assets/images/MF_Sigmoid.avif")} alt="" createdIn="2026">
	The **factor** defaults to `1`. To properly handle negative numbers, you should just be able to
	put an **Abs** node between the **input value** and the **Add** node.
</MainImage>

#### Facilitating Facial Animations

In the music video, we just stare at Paige's back the entire time. However, this very article's hero

I used
[this tutorial](https://dev.epicgames.com/community/learning/tutorials/k8y8/editing-materials-with-control-rig)
for animating materials using a control rig. This approach calls for very specific material
parameters, so I needed to create some materials for each facial feature that needed to be animated,
each with unique parameter names. Here's what one of them looks like:

<MainImage src={import("./assets/images/M_Paige_Mouth.avif")} alt="" createdIn="2026" />

Keep in mind each spritesheet for Paige's facial features has a different number of sprites in them.
The **B** value of the **Divide** node needs to be changed depending on how many of them it has.
Here it's 16 because the mouth texture has 16 sprites on each axis, but for the eyes it needs to be
8, and 4 for the eyebrows.

### It's Sequencer Time!

##### Avoiding Audio Anomalies

The Sequencer allows us to drag any imported audio file directly into it. That's quite neat, however
one problem I ran into is that the audio would de-sync more and more while playing. Not exactly
ideal when you're trying to make a music video.

You can set the Sequencer's clock source to **Audio** to try and combat this:

<MainImage src={import("./assets/images/Sequencer-AudioClock.avif")} alt="" createdIn="2026" />

{/* The <wbr /> here is to fix the console command overflowing on mobile */}

And while this does fix the de-syncing, for some reason, it makes the audio crackly instead.
Thankfully I found
[a solution to this](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/ue5-sequencer-audio-de-sync-issue-with-clock-set-to-tick-cracking-popping-when-clock-set-to-audio/548281)
on the forums, namely the console command <kbd>Sequencer.Audio.<wbr />UseAudioClockForAudioDesync
1</kbd>. Combining this with the audio clock makes it behave pretty much perfectly for
audio-sensitive workflows like this.

<Alert type="tip">
	You can throw this command in a
	[configuration file](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/configuration-files-in-unreal-engine?application_version=5.7#setconsolevariablesinconfigurationfiles) 
	if you want it to be enabled by default. I ended up adding it to Unreal Editor's
	[`ConsoleVariables.ini`](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/console-variables-cplusplus-in-unreal-engine?application_version=5.7#loadingconsolevariables):

```ini
[Startup]
Sequencer.Audio.UseAudioClockForAudioDesync = 1
```

</Alert>

##### Sequencer-driven Materials

Normally, if you want a material to evolve over time, you would use the appropriately named **Time**
node, which just outputs the world time in seconds. However, I wanted the sky to move in sync with
the Sequencer.

I tried looking up how to do this, but what I found back then was that you had to use what's called
a **material parameter collection** to animate materials. Now, that may have been the case back in
the UE4 days, but while researching a similar topic in late August, I accidentally found out that
[you CAN animate material parameters](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/animate-materials-in-unreal-engine-cinematic?application_version=5.7#animatematerialparameters).
In fact, it's been possible since at least Unreal Engine 5.0.

However, I didn't know this at the time, and in fact, using a material parameter collection has one
big benefit: you're able to re-use it across multiple materials. As such, it is probably the best
approach for this use case.

So, what I did is I created one of these collections, containing nothing more than a scalar **Time**
parameter. It's so simple I don't even need to show it to you. Then I added it to the Sequencer and
made it so it linearly increases by 1 every second, so that it would effectively hold the sequence's
current time in seconds:

#### Exporting the Video

Phew. We finally have our music video. Now we just need to render it. That shouldn't be too hard,
right?

_\*sighs\*_ You wish. I used the Movie Render Queue for this, and while it shows an option to export
an H.264 video, it simply isn't supported Linux. In fact, none of the video export modes are. _Once
again, us Linux users are being left in the dark._

Not all hope is lost, though. We can still export an image sequence. For the first test renders I
did, I used a simple `.png` sequence, but even at a reduced resolution and framerate, it was still
taking up tens of gigabytes of disk space, which is not ideal. There is an option to use a `.jpg`
sequence, but I don't trust it.

Instead, I switched to an `.exr` sequence with `DWAB` compression. While this is lossy, you honestly
can't even tell. That still ended up with a size of about 20 gigabytes, but it's definitely less
than what a PNG sequence would've been at 4K 60fps. And we also get some... advantages?

You see, these EXR images store 16-bit **_linear_** colours. That's fine for professional work,
especially because you get much more brightness detail than the 8-bit images from PNG sequences,
even with the lossy compression. They may even be suitable for HDR work, but I wasn't able to figure
that out. One big downside though is that if you try to use them directly, the end result will be
way too dark.

Now is a good time to discuss how I turned this image sequence into a proper video. Unreal Engine
does have an option to use a
[command-line encoder](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/cinematic-rendering-export-formats-in-unreal-engine?application_version=5.7#commandlineencoder),
but my install of FFmpeg refused to cooperate, so I instead just executed FFmpeg directly:

```bash
ffmpeg -hide_banner -y \
	-hwaccel vaapi -hwaccel_output_format vaapi -vaapi_device /dev/dri/renderD128 \
	-framerate 60 -i './stargazer-music-video_final/%04d.exr' -i 'f78 - gaze upon the stars.flac' \
	-c:a copy -c:v hevc_vaapi -b:v 50M -tier 1 -vf 'zscale=t=iec61966-2-1,format=nv12,hwupload' \
	'stargazer-v2_final.mkv'
```

Let's dissect this step by step.

1. `-hide_banner` hides the incredibly big banner, and `-y` always overwrites the output file if it
   already exists, instead of asking. This is just for convenience, so don't worry about it.
2. `-hwaccel vaapi -hwaccel_output_format vaapi -vaapi_device /dev/dri/renderD128` tells FFmpeg that
   we want to use VAAPI for hardware acceleration. This is what worked for me on Linux with my AMD
   GPU. For more information, refer to the [FFmpeg wiki](https://trac.ffmpeg.org/wiki/HWAccelIntro).
3. `-framerate 60` specifes the input framerate. This is required as we're dealing with an image
   sequence.
4. `-i './stargazer-music-video_final/%04d.exr'` passes said image sequence as an input, using a
   pattern.
5. `-i 'f78 - gaze upon the stars.flac'` then specifies the original song as an input. While you can
   export audio from Unreal Engine, it is not necessary, since the only audio track is the song
   itself. It can also break, too.
6. `-c:a copy` tells FFmpeg to not encode the audio, and just add it to the video container
   directly. YouTube recommends FLAC audio for music videos anyway.
7. `-c:v hevc_vaapi -b:v 50M -tier1` specifies HEVC (H.265) as the video codec, with the High tier,
   a target bitrate of 50Mbps (which was overkill, it only needed 20Mbps), and using the VAAPI
   hardware acceleration from earlier. While CRF would be better, it's unfortunately not supported
   by VAAPI.
8. `-vf 'zscale=t=iec61966-2-1,format=nv12,hwupload'` specifies the following video filters:
   1. **`zscale=t=iec61966-2-1` is the important part.** It's what converts the linear values from
      our EXR sequence to sRGB, which looked the most accurate to me.
   2. `format=nv12,hwupload` is some more VAAPI hardware acceleration stuff.
9. Now we're just left with `'stargazer-v2_final.mkv'`, which is the output path.

And at last, we have a video file. Now we just upload it to YouTube, and... there's a slight
problem. You see, YouTube uses the BT.709 colour space, so it ended up mangling our sRGB colours a
bit. The end result is that the video looks a bit darker than it's supposed to. Thankfully we can
just call that a feature, since, again, it is nighttime. I'll try to figure it out by the time I
finish the next music video.
